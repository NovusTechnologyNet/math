# Primes settle pi's formulas: publish that first

Yes. In 2026 an amateur working with an AI system and one strong laptop can realistically publish on pi and on primes, and the most publishable result is already in this project. Theorem A says that every rational linear relation between pi and the binary generators arctan(1/2^m) and arctan(1/(2^m − 1)) comes from three classical identities. It does not appear anywhere in the literature this research could reach, and it directly answers Research Problem (2), which Borwein, Borwein and Galway (BBG) left open in 2004. Its proof is short and built from known parts: BBG's own "private prime" method, plus Schinzel's 1962 theorem for the lemma on Aurifeuillian factors. On its own it is a solid short paper, not a breakthrough. The larger, riskier and more valuable package is Parts III and IV of the note. They turn the unexplained zero-relation counts in Bailey's BBP compendium into theorems and prove the 7/24 optimum, but nobody independent has checked them yet. Three presentation faults must be fixed before any submission. Lemma 2 must be credited to Schinzel. The dimension counts must be framed as a proof of Bailey's published table, not a discovery. And the new formula must be called optimal only under the project's own per-prime cost, because under the published measure (non-zero terms per bit) it is slower than Bellard's and Huvent's formulas. The GPU program's real contribution is window length: a million hexadecimal digits, where published far-position runs give at most about a thousand bits. It is not a digit-extraction record or a route to the all-digits record. The famous problems (normality of pi, twin primes, Goldbach, Legendre) remain out of reach. In 2026 the frontier on the Riemann Hypothesis, bounded prime gaps and pi's irrationality exponent was taken by AI labs using unreleased models. The Lean community accepts Levent Alpöge's Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as formally correct, but humans do not yet understand it, it has not been peer reviewed, and the Clay Institute has not recognized it. The recommended path has six steps: fix the attributions, get Parts III and IV reviewed independently, formalize Theorem A in Lean on top of an existing proof of Bang's theorem (the Aurifeuillian lemma would be the first formal proof of its kind), deposit on Zenodo, reach arXiv through one personal endorsement, and submit to a journal whose editors know the 2004 paper.

## In 2026 the famous problems moved toward the AI labs, not toward amateurs

**On pi, the deep questions are as closed as ever.** Nobody has proved that pi is normal in any base, or even that every decimal digit occurs infinitely often. No irrational algebraic number is known to be normal ([Wikipedia: Normal number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_number)). Whether pi and e are algebraically independent, and whether pi + e is irrational, are still open; the best result in that direction is still Nesterenko's theorem that pi and e^pi are algebraically independent ([arXiv:1310.7289](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.7289v3)).

The all-digits record is an engineering frontier, not a mathematical one. It stands at **314 trillion decimal digits, computed with y-cruncher and the Chudnovsky series on a single Dell server with about 2.1 PB of NVMe flash, in about 110 days ending 18-19 November 2025** ([StorageReview](https://www.storagereview.com/review/storagereview-sets-new-pi-record-314-trillion-digits-on-a-dell-poweredge-r7725)). The y-cruncher records page, updated 25 October 2026, lists nothing larger ([y-cruncher records](https://numberworld.org/y-cruncher/records.html)). Alexander Yee, y-cruncher's author, describes such runs as limited by storage and data movement, and says development is on hiatus ([y-cruncher news 2025](https://numberworld.org/y-cruncher/news/2025.html)).

The one pi question that moved is the irrationality exponent. The refereed bound of 7.1032 (Zeilberger and Zudilin, 2020) ([arXiv:1912.06345](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06345)) was lowered to 7.1019 in a September 2026 single-author preprint that discloses heavy help from OpenAI models ([arXiv:2609.11276](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11276)). OpenAI then posted a preprint claiming the exponent is exactly 2 ([OpenAI preprint](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/The-irrationality-exponent-of-pi-is-2-September-24-2026/paper.pdf)). MathWorld, updated 20 November 2026, records that no independent specialist had verified the full proof as of 7 October, and it keeps 7.1032 as the established bound ([MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/IrrationalityMeasure.html)). The exponent-2 claim should be treated as unverified.

**On primes, the Riemann Hypothesis now has a machine-checked proof that no human yet understands.** On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge posted a four-page note saying that, working with Claude, he had found a proof of RH. The proof is supplied only as Lean code, about 17 million lines; his footnote says a prose version ran past 250,000 pages ([Alpöge, Remark](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf)). On 21 November the Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, saying they are fully confident the proof is correct. The proved theorem is Mathlib's own `RiemannHypothesis`, dozens of people reproduced the build, and several independent checkers accepted the exported proof ([Lean community statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/)). Andrew Sutherland's verification page lists five theorems: RH, plus GRH for every Dirichlet L-function. They depend only on Lean's three standard axioms. They do not cover Dedekind, Hecke or general automorphic L-functions, and at its last update (12:55 UTC on 21 November) some kernel runs were still going ([Sutherland](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)). Both pages were checked directly against the primary sources for this report.

What has not happened matters just as much. There is no human-readable proof and no peer review. Terence Tao says digesting the proof will take time and that it appears to use the full strength of the modern Langlands program ([Tao, 20 Nov 2026](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/20/unforced-euler-singularity-unforced-navier-stokes-singularity-and-the-generalized-riemann-hypothesis/)). The Clay Institute still lists RH as unsolved ([Clay Mathematics Institute](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/riemann-hypothesis/)).

The rest of the prime frontier shows the same pattern. OpenAI's October "quasi-Riemann" release (all zeros with real part between 1/12 and 11/12) is largely accepted by the experts who studied it, though they called its exposition well below the field's standards ([Quanta](https://quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019)). Bounded prime gaps moved fastest. **Julia Stadlmann's human improvement from 246 to 240 (31 August 2026) was overtaken by Axiom Math's 212 about three days later, and by OpenAI's 186 on 3 September**; none of these has been peer reviewed ([Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/human-math-record-twin-primes-openai)).

Several other records are out of reach of a single machine:

- **Goldbach:** binary Goldbach is verified only up to 4 × 10^18, a 2014 result ([Math. Comp. 2014](https://www.ams.org/journals/mcom/2014-83-288/S0025-5718-2013-02787-1)). The fastest published single-GPU code checks up to 10^13 in 3,246 s on an RTX 5090 ([arXiv:2603.07850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07850)). At that rate, simply reaching the existing record would take about 41 years.
- **Legendre's conjecture:** it does not follow even from RH ([arXiv:2602.22502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22502)), and its latest numerical verification took half a year on 256 cores ([arXiv:2401.13753](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.13753)).
- **A new Mersenne prime:** the last one, M136279841 (October 2024), used thousands of cloud GPUs at a reported cost of about $2 million ([Wikipedia: Largest known prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_known_prime_number)). None has followed in 2025 or 2026 ([GIMPS](https://www.mersenne.org/)).

**If the Lean RH proof stands, it changes what is worth doing on primes, though less than it seems.** Any theorem about primes proved "assuming RH" becomes unconditional once that deduction is itself checked. A third party derived Robin's and Lagarias's inequalities as unconditional Lean corollaries within a day ([Sutherland](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/)). Twin primes, Goldbach, Legendre and n² + 1 do not follow from RH. The primes thread notes, as background knowledge it did not re-verify, that Hooley's conditional proof of Artin's conjecture needs GRH for Dedekind zeta functions, which the Lean proof does not cover. Much explicit analytic number theory exists only because RH was unproven, so its value shifts toward recording and formalizing the conditional-to-unconditional deductions. Specialists do that within weeks of each new input: Fiori and Jaskari published bounds built on OpenAI's zero-free claim almost immediately ([arXiv:2609.23222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23222)).

## Bounded, checkable projects are where independent authors still publish

**For pi, the repeatable task that works for amateurs is proving a formula someone has already conjectured.** The 2026 Ramanujan Challenge drew 228 submissions, including some from amateurs, a person without higher mathematical education and a high-school student. 54 of 137 natural-language proofs were judged correct. Proofs backed by computer-algebra verification were right 52% of the time, against 6% for proofs backed only by numerical checks. One published open conjecture (an integral equal to 4π²/85) received a correct Lean-formalized proof ([arXiv:2610.40551](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.40551)).

The supply of targets is large. Zhi-Wei Sun's lists contain 234 conjectural series, 178 of them for 1/pi ([arXiv:1102.5649](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.5649)). The conservative-matrix-field work found that 43% of 385 validated pi formulas descend from one object, 51% sit in clusters not yet resolved and 6% are orphans ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/mathematicians-find-one-pi-formula-to-rule-them-all/); [arXiv:2502.17533](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17533)). Kam Cheong Au, who shows no affiliation on arXiv, proved several such conjectures by Wilf-Zeilberger (WZ) methods in the Journal of Symbolic Computation ([arXiv:2312.14051](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.14051)).

Other pi projects are within reach:

- **A Lean proof of the Chudnovsky formula.** It is an open item on the lean-eval leaderboard ([lean-eval](https://lean-lang.org/eval/problems/chudnovsky_formula_for_pi_inv/)), and Milla has already written out a detailed proof that can be followed ([arXiv:1809.00533](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.00533v2)).
- **Digit statistics.** Published statistics stop at 22.4 trillion decimal digits ([arXiv:1612.00489](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00489)), even though the 314-trillion-digit dataset (about 130 TB) can now be retrieved from Backblaze ([Backblaze](https://www.backblaze.com/blog/backblaze-now-serving-314-trillion-digits-of-pi/)). A journal would want a new statistic, not just more digits.
- **Continued-fraction statistics.** 653.52 billion terms of pi's continued fraction were computed in 2025, and the Khinchin and Lévy limits are unproven for pi ([MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PiContinuedFraction.html)).
- **Prime prefixes of pi.** No new term has been found since the 16,208-digit probable prime of 2001 ([OEIS A005042](https://oeis.org/A005042)). This is a registry contribution, not a paper.
- **Fast series for other constants.** An independent civil engineer, Jorge Zuniga, found the fastest known series for log 2, log 3 and log 5, and y-cruncher adopted them ([StorageReview guest post](https://www.storagereview.com/review/the-math-of-performance-stress-testing-hardware-for-math-changing-results)). This is the clearest precedent for an amateur changing real computational practice.

**For primes, the best laptop-plus-GPU targets are under-searched special-prime families.** The Wieferich search in base 2 reached 2^64, about 1.8 × 10^19, through a PrimeGrid project that ended in December 2022 and used per-interval checksums for double-checking. Earlier volunteer runs beyond 10^17 were judged unreliable and wasted ([Wikipedia: Wieferich prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wieferich_prime)). In bases 3 to 30 the equivalent search stops at 5 × 10^13 ([Wikipedia: Fermat quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_quotient)), a gap of more than five orders of magnitude. Pell-Wieferich primes are searched only to 10^9 and tribonacci-Wieferich primes to 10^11 ([Wikipedia: Wall-Sun-Sun prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall%E2%80%93Sun%E2%80%93Sun_prime)). Wilson primes stop at 2 × 10^13 (2014) ([Wikipedia: Wilson prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilson_prime)), and Wolstenholme primes at 10^11 (2022) ([Wikipedia: Wolstenholme prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolstenholme_prime)).

A search of the form "we searched X up to B with method M, double-checked it, and found the following" is publishable whether or not a new prime turns up. Dorais and Klyve's Journal of Integer Sequences paper of 2011 is the template. The GoldbachGPU episode shows why the double-check matters: its second version corrected two concurrency defects and withdrew its four-GPU results ([arXiv:2603.07850](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07850)).

Record lists credit individuals by name but rarely lead to papers. The largest known maximal prime gap, 1,854 after a prime near 1.01 × 10^20, was found in 2026 by Robert Smith running Brian Kehrig's code ([Wikipedia: Prime gap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_gap); [MathWorld](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PrimeGaps.html)). A 3.7-million-digit generalized Fermat prime was found in 2025 on a single RTX 4080 SUPER ([PrimeGrid](https://www.primegrid.com/)). Formalization projects such as PNT+ publish claimable tasks ([PrimeNumberTheoremAnd](https://github.com/AlexKontorovich/PrimeNumberTheoremAnd)).

**The project already sits on one of the few live bridges between pi and primes.** The "pi and primes" links people usually cite (Euler's ζ(2) = π²/6, the probability 6/π² that two integers are coprime) are classical, and the pi thread found no active research program reaching pi through primes ([Wikipedia: Pi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pi)). In this project, by contrast, which binary formulas for pi exist is decided by primitive prime divisors: primes dividing 2^n − 1, or one Aurifeuillian factor of 4^n + 1, and no smaller term of the same kind. For odd n those Aurifeuillian factors are exactly the norms of the Gaussian Mersenne numbers (1+i)^n ∓ 1. So Lemma 2 is, word for word, a theorem about primitive prime divisors of Gaussian Mersenne numbers.

The primes thread found no 2023-2026 paper on primitive divisors of Aurifeuillian factors or in the Gaussian integers. That is weak evidence, because zbMATH and MathSciNet could not be queried. Two natural follow-ups for the user's second passion come out of this:

- an exception-list theorem saying that each Aurifeuillian factor in the Cunningham bases has its own primitive prime divisor, checked against Wagstaff's tables and his 2012 search for unexplained near-equal splits ([Wagstaff, Integers 2012](https://homes.cerias.purdue.edu/~ssw/cun/mine.pdf));
- a partial GPU search of the Gaussian Mersenne exponents between 5,000,000 (the exhaustive limit) and 15,317,227 (the largest known exponent, whose rank is uncertain) ([OEIS A057429](https://oeis.org/A057429)).

**The 2026 record of AI-assisted amateurs is a guide to what referees will accept.** Liam Price, 23 and without advanced training, got a proof of Erdős problem #1196 from GPT-5.4 Pro, and the resulting paper has Tao and Lichtman among its authors ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/amateur-armed-with-chatgpt-vibe-maths-a-60-year-old-problem/); [arXiv:2605.00301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00301)). The same pair's earlier claim on #333 turned out to have been settled by Erdős in 1977 ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-ai-tore-through-a-mathematical-community-20260803/)). A DeepMind agent then resolved 9 of 353 open Erdős problems at a few hundred dollars each ([arXiv:2605.22763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22763)), so the remaining problems are now selected for resisting exactly the tools an amateur would use. Thomas Bloom describes non-mathematicians posting 100-200 page AI-generated papers that no human has read ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-ai-tore-through-a-mathematical-community-20260803/)). Substantive AI disclosure in arXiv mathematics submissions rose from 1.39% in March to 14.09% in August 2026 ([arXiv:2608.24961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.24961)). The results that were received well share a profile: a precisely stated, catalogued question; a literature check; a formal proof or a professional's sign-off; and a short write-up that a human has digested.

Ranked by the chance of a refereed outcome on this hardware (a judgment built on the evidence above), the options are:

1. the project's classification paper;
2. a companion paper on the window algorithm;
3. a Lean formalization of Theorem A;
4. an Aurifeuillian or Gaussian Mersenne primitive-divisor note;
5. a double-checked generalized Wieferich search;
6. proofs of catalogued pi identities.

## The classification answers a question open since 2004, built from known parts

**Theorem A is new as a statement and short as a proof.** BBG proved that pi has no Machin-type BBP arctangent formula in any base other than 2. For base 2 they found the three formulas R1-R3 and two zero relations by a hand search. In their Appendix D they then asked: "How many Q-linearly-independent binary Machin-type BBP arctangent zero relations exist?" ([BBG 2004](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-mathematics/article/finding-and-excluding-bary-machintype-individual-digit-formulae/BB7919C8E1AE66878AE8D7BC7F0EBFA3)). In their notation, Theorem A says the answer is exactly two: their equations (35) and (36), which the arctangent thread checked are R2 − R1 and R3 − R1. Corollary 1 settles their Problem (1), about the duplicate series for arctan(1/3). Of the roughly 25 works that cite BBG, none takes these problems up ([Semantic Scholar citation record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.4153/CJM-2004-041-2/citations?fields=title,year,authors,externalIds,venue&limit=200)). Wikipedia, MathWorld, Arndt's arctangent pages and Lafont's OEIS wiki page do not contain the statement either.

The ingredients, however, are all in print:

- The two-term case is Størmer's classical theorem, completed by Ljunggren in 1942 ([Calcut 2009](https://www2.oberlin.edu/faculty/jcalcut/gausspi.pdf)).
- The private-prime argument is the one BBG themselves use for their Theorem 7, which proves the case b ≠ 2 ([BBG 2004 PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/BB7919C8E1AE66878AE8D7BC7F0EBFA3/S0008414X0003385Xa.pdf/finding-and-excluding-b-ary-machin-type-individual-digit-formulae.pdf)).
- Lemma 2 is, in substance, Schinzel's 1962 theorem on primitive prime factors of a^n − b^n, specialized to a = 2, b = 1 ([Schinzel 1962](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/mathematical-proceedings-of-the-cambridge-philosophical-society/article/on-primitive-prime-factors-of-anbn/FF4F8CB4D5BEDD2854151670559F36C6)). Stewart's account shows that Schinzel proved such numbers have two primitive divisors by going through an Aurifeuillian factorization ([Stewart, arXiv:1008.1274](https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.1274)).
- The cyclotomic factorization behind Lemma 2 is printed as Example 3.2 in Chamberland's 2003 paper ([Chamberland 2003](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/VOL6/Chamberland/chamberland60.pdf)).

There is also an insight a referee will value. BBG's Problem (7) pointed to Schinzel's 1974 theorem and to Bilu-Hanrot-Voutier as the tools for Q(i). The arctangent thread found that neither suffices on its own: Bilu-Hanrot-Voutier does not apply to the pair (1+i, 1), and Schinzel 1974 leaves an astronomically large unchecked range. It is Schinzel 1962, absent from BBG's reference list, that closes the binary case.

The nearest recent paper, Gasull, Luca and Varona (2023), classifies only two-term formulas with arguments of the form 2^a/b or b/2^a ([arXiv:2302.00154](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.00154)). It cites neither BBG nor Bailey, so a paper citing all three would bridge two literatures that ignore each other. The honest verdict: the gap is real and dated, but a referee may call Theorem A a corollary of BBG's method plus Schinzel 1962. That makes it a short paper on its own, and much stronger when bundled with what follows.

**Theorems B to F are the stronger contribution, and they explain a table Bailey called not understood.** Bailey's compendium (version of 8 April 2023) tabulates linearly independent zero relations found by PSLQ, a numerical relation-finding algorithm, for 27 binary degree-1 cases. The counts are 1, 5, 3 and 7 at base 16 with length 8, base 2^12 with length 24, base 2^20 with length 40 and base 2^60 with length 120, and there are no rows beyond length 120. Bailey states that it is not understood why zero relations occur for some parameters and not others. The only explanation he credits is Broadhurst's unpublished 2000 manuscript, available only from its author ([Bailey compendium](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/bbp-formulas.pdf)).

Theorem C's pi-or-zero spaces at those periods have dimensions 2, 6, 4 and 8. That is exactly Bailey's counts plus one dimension for pi, and the period-120 ceiling matches the absence of longer rows. The project's lattice-reduction run outside the family (n = 4, period 240) reproduced dimension 8 and the 7/24 optimum by a method that uses no roots of unity and no Baker theorem (`outside_family_findings.md`).

The surrounding literature constructs relations but never claims completeness:

- Lafont proved eight of the relations in 2011 and made no completeness claim ([Lafont 2011](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/zero-relations/13076965)).
- Barsky, Muñoz and Pérez-Marco asked whether all degree-1 null formulas arise from their construction ([arXiv:1906.09629](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.09629)).
- Kristensen and Mathiasen wrote that they may not have found all zero formulas ([arXiv:2208.07696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.07696)).

No source found uses Baker's theorem on BBP-type series, says that only finitely many roots of unity can occur, or names period 120 as the end. One caution: the BBP thread recalls, without being able to verify it, that Baker, Birch and Wirsing (1973) and Adhikari, Saradha, Shorey and Tijdeman (2001) applied Baker's theorem to sums with periodic coefficients. If so, Step 1 of Part III is a known technique applied to a new family, and those papers must be cited.

**The optimality theorem is new only together with its new cost measure.** The published cost measure for BBP formulas is the number of non-zero terms per bit ([arXiv:1906.09629](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.09629)). Bellard left open in 1997 whether a formula faster than his exists ([Bellard 1997](https://www.bellard.org/pi/pi_bin/pi_bin.html)). Huvent minimized term counts in 2001 inside a parametrized family, but only heuristically, presenting his choices as apparently best rather than proved ([Huvent 2001](https://c8cf35d7-ad1f-4031-84c6-bb15d656c101.filesusr.com/ugd/a97b48_b22c37a8b999457f85fe6d8528582dfa.pdf)). y-cruncher made Huvent's formula its default digit extractor in version 0.8.5, in July 2024 ([y-cruncher version history](https://www.numberworld.org/y-cruncher/version_history.html)). Nobody defines cost by the first multiple of each prime that enters a denominator, and nobody proves an optimum under any measure.

The two measures rank formulas in opposite orders. The first two value columns are computed in the BBP thread from the cited formulas; the state column multiplies by 2.88, as the project note does (the Bellard and Huvent values are derived here).

| Formula | Terms per bit (published measure) | mean(1/s) (project's prime measure) | Per-prime state, bits per bit of position |
|---|---|---|---|
| BBP 1995 | 1.000 | 3/4 | 2.16 |
| Bellard 1997 | 0.700 | 1/2 | 1.44 |
| Hutton/Euler (Bailey's Formula 19) | 0.700 | 1/3 | 0.96 |
| Huvent 2001 (y-cruncher default since 2024) | 0.667 | 5/12 | 1.20 |
| New formula, Theorem E | 0.917 | 7/24 | 0.84 |

Measured on the user's laptop (Core Ultra 9 275HX, RTX 5090 Laptop GPU), the new formula uses **12.0% fewer primes at position 10^10**. It reads **1,000,000 hex digits at position 10^10 in 80.5 s, against 92.8 s for Euler's formula**, but gains only 4-6% on 64-digit windows. Every output matched stored reference digits, and the published digits at 10^11 were reproduced (`pi_formula_theorem.md`).

**The window program's novelty is length, not position or speed per digit.** Splitting a digit-extraction sum by primes is the device of Plouffe (1996) and Bellard (1997) for decimal digits, at roughly quadratic cost ([arXiv:0912.0303](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.0303); [Bellard O(n²)](https://www.bellard.org/pi/pi_n2/pi_n2.html)). Every binary implementation the BBP thread could read evaluates the series term by term ([Sze, arXiv:1008.3171](https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.3171)). Published far-position runs return short windows: 152 bits at the trillionth bit (Bellard), 1,000 bits at 10^13 (Sze), and 32 hex digits at 10^17 (Takahashi, December 2016), the farthest position documented ([Takahashi](https://www.hpcs.cs.tsukuba.ac.jp/~daisuke/pihex.html)).

For short windows the program has no clear claim over the published state of the art. y-cruncher's 2018 hex check near position 2.6 × 10^13 took about 20 hours on a 10-core desktop CPU ([Yee 2019](https://www.numberworld.org/blogs/2019_3_14_pi_record/)). Scaled linearly, that is the same order as the project's 107-114 s for 64 hex digits at 10^11.

What has no published counterpart is a million-digit window. Position 10^10 lies far inside the record's known digits (314 trillion decimal digits is about 2.6 × 10^14 hex digits), so those digits are verification, not new digits. One inference about where a long window would matter: y-cruncher's record rules for pi accept a single Chudnovsky run with hex output, checked by a BBP spot check of the final hex digits ([y-cruncher records](https://numberworld.org/y-cruncher/records.html)). A long-window tool could check far more of those final digits. The note itself says the program is not a route to an all-digits record (`pi_formula_theorem.md`), and the project's own all-digits test found windows 23-27 times slower than y-cruncher on the same laptop. The "nothing found" behind this program's novelty rests on only two targeted searches, so it is the weakest part of the novelty check.

## Five fixes and three additions make the notes refereeable

**First, credit the known parts precisely.** Read Schinzel 1962 before submission; it is paywalled and nobody in this project has seen its exact hypotheses or its list of exceptions. The note currently recalls the exceptions from memory. Lemma 2 should be stated as Schinzel's theorem, with the self-contained proof offered as a re-derivation. Cite Chamberland for the factorization, Størmer for the two-term case and BBG's Theorem 7 for the method. Check Steidl, Hänler and Tasche (1989), whose title promises a Zsigmondy theorem in quadratic integer rings and which may be a second source for Lemma 2 ([Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF01194869)).

Fix the naming. Calcut, following Tweddle, credits all three classical two-term formulas to Machin in 1706 ([Calcut 2009](https://www2.oberlin.edu/faculty/jcalcut/gausspi.pdf)). Bailey's Formula 19 is the binary series of Hutton's identity, ascribed to Pschill in *Pi Unleashed* and traced to Euler by Craig-Wood ([Bailey compendium](https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/bbp-formulas.pdf)). Calling it simply "Euler's formula" will draw a referee's comment. Also correct the note's claim that Bellard's formula was found by adding a zero series. That route is documented for the 1995 BBP formula (Ferguson's formula is BBP plus a quarter of zero relation 111); Bellard derived his formula from a Machin-type relation.

**Second, frame every result against the question it answers.** Theorem A answers BBG Problems (1) and (2). Parts III and IV answer Problem (10) for pi in base 2. Theorem C is the proof of Bailey's Table 1, so the paper should derive all 27 binary degree-1 rows from Theorem C and print the comparison. That is both the strongest validation and an honest statement that the counts were known. Bailey's compendium already summarizes BBG as excluding all other degree-1 formulas, which is stronger than what BBG prove; the literature half-believes the full result, so present it as confirming and explaining an expectation. Write to Broadhurst about his unpublished manuscript before claiming the first structural account.

**Third, present the cost measure honestly.** Give both columns of the cost table. State that the new formula is slower than Bellard's and Huvent's under the standard measure. Compare it with Huvent's formula, not only with Euler's, because Huvent's is what practitioners use. Every "optimal" must carry its qualifier: among binary degree-1 BBP-type series, under per-prime state.

**Fourth, close the verification gap.** Parts III and IV were written in one sitting and checked only by the project's own programs. The note's earlier claim that logarithm-type terms cannot help was false, and only the adversarial review caught it, which shows that review finds real errors here. Before submission:

- Run an independent review of the per-root argument in Step 2, the valuation table and the Kummer irreducibility step.
- Replace the floating-point optimality search with an exact enumeration of the 6,724,520 candidates.
- State that the lattice tables cannot see relations with coefficients above about 2^25.

**Fifth, write the paper in the author's own words, with an AI declaration.** The working notes speak in an AI's voice ("a second run of the same model"). The Journal of Integer Sequences forbids LLM-written text ([JIS](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/)), and Integers requires that the wording be the authors' own ([Integers](https://math.colgate.edu/~integers/submit.html)). Every publisher checked forbids AI authorship and requires disclosure; the LMS has required an "AI Declaration" section in every manuscript since October 2026 ([LMS](https://www.lms.ac.uk/publications/policies/AI)). The criticism of the RH release was precisely that its author does not fully understand it. Theorem A is short enough to master completely, and a cover letter from someone who can explain every step reads very differently from one who cannot. Henry Cohn's advice applies directly: referees should not be the first readers, and an introduction should explain the topic and its relevance without bragging ([Cohn](https://cohn.mit.edu/advice)).

**Three additions would strengthen the paper and tie it to practice:**

- **The standard-measure optimum.** Is Huvent's 8 terms per 12 bits the minimum over all binary degree-1 formulas? The same vertex enumeration over the 8-dimensional space, with a different objective, should settle it. A proved answer would matter more to implementers than 7/24, because Huvent's formula is y-cruncher's default.
- **The orphan arctangents.** Bailey still lists as open whether arctan(2/7) has a binary BBP formula, and BBG's Theorem 2(b) excludes only non-Aurifeuillian formulas for 2/7, 4/9 and 5/9. The arctangent thread sketches a private-prime argument, not yet checked by computer: 53 = 2² + 7² and 2 has order 52 modulo 53, which would settle 2/7 inside the Machin-type class. That would be a second concrete answer to a published open item.
- **One sentence on CORDIC.** Theorem A contains the linear independence over Q of the CORDIC angles arctan(2^(−k)) for k ≥ 2, together with pi. No statement of this was found, and readers in hardware and numerical analysis will recognize it.

This report synthesizes seven research threads that together ran **200 web searches**: state of pi (42), state of primes (29), AI-assisted mathematics (21), formalization (30), venues and process (41), novelty of the arctangent classification (20), and novelty of the BBP theory and algorithms (17). ## Conclusion

The research changes the picture in one important way: the user's two passions are not separate projects. The question "which binary formulas for pi exist?" is answered by the arithmetic of primitive primes in 2^n − 1 and in the Gaussian Mersenne numbers (1+i)^n ∓ 1. The most promising continuations keep that bridge: Huvent's optimality, the orphan arctangent 2/7, Aurifeuillian exception lists for the Cunningham bases, and the unsearched Gaussian Mersenne range. A pi result here is a primes result, and a formal proof of Schinzel's Aurifeuillian lemma would be a contribution to both.

The second lesson concerns where an amateur's advantage now lies. In 2026, producing proofs stopped being the bottleneck: labs produced proofs of RH, quasi-RH and bounded gaps faster than humans could read them, and the RH proof drew criticism precisely for being correct but not understood. What the field rewards, and what labs supply least, is a short, precisely positioned answer to a question someone actually asked, checked by a machine and explained by a human who understands it. The project has that profile. It will reach print if it is presented as the completion of Borwein, Borwein and Galway's 2004 programme and as the proof of Bailey's table, rather than as a faster way to compute pi.
